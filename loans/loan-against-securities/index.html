What is a loan against securities?

A loan against securities lets you borrow against investments you already own, such as listed shares, mutual funds, bonds and some insurance policies, while keeping the investments. The lender puts a lien or pledge on the securities and lends a percentage of their value, called the margin.

This is a good bridge when you need short-term funds but do not want to sell long-term holdings, trigger capital-gains tax, or lose market upside. Because market prices move, lenders can ask for additional security or part-repayment if values fall. NEXUS helps you identify which holdings are eligible and which lenders to approach.

Eligibility criteria

Each lender sets its own rules. These are typical conditions:

  • Securities from the lender's approved list
  • Demat account and KYC in order
  • Age 18+ for individuals, entity KYC for companies
  • Minimum loan size set by the lender

Documents required

Keep these ready to avoid delays. Your lender may ask for more depending on your profile. Use our document checklist tool for a printable list.

  • PAN, Aadhaar and address proof
  • Demat holding statement or mutual fund account statement
  • Bank statement and income proof, where requested
  • Pledge forms and lien-marking confirmation

Fees and charges to look out for

  • Processing fee: typically 0.25% to 1% of the limit
  • Pledge creation and invocation charges
  • Annual renewal fee on overdraft limits

Smart tips before you apply

  • Know the lender's margin call rules before pledging
  • Keep a buffer; a market fall can trigger a call for additional security
  • Avoid pledging all holdings
  • Check whether dividends and bonus units stay with you

Frequently asked questions

Which securities can be pledged?
Lenders maintain approved lists that usually include large-cap shares, selected mutual funds and bonds. Eligibility is lender-specific.
What is a margin call?
If the value of your pledged securities falls below the required level, the lender asks you to add collateral or repay part of the loan. If you do not, the lender can sell securities.
Is it cheaper than a personal loan?
Often yes, because it is secured, but market risk makes it unsuitable for borrowers who cannot absorb a fall.
Can I pledge insurance policies?
Some lenders accept surrender-value policies. Terms vary by policy and insurer.
